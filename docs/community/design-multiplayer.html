Multiplayer

status: charted, the farthest sail on this chart; the foundations are being laid now

One sea, shared. The plan is that someday you open openpiece.io, press Sail, and the living world every other page describes has other real captains on it: crews of actual people, rivals with someone behind the eyes, wars where both admirals are players.

One law above all of it: the world stays exactly as honest with a hundred players as it is with one. Same simulation, same rules, nobody special.

The front door

Two buttons. Quick Join drops you into a healthy public sea with room aboard. Browse shows every public sea like a harbor board: its name, captains currently aboard, how old its world is, its house rules. Click one and you're sailing, in your browser, no install. The ease of the little web games you already know, with a living world behind it instead of an arena.

Seas belong to their communities

Servers are not ours. The server is a free program anyone can run: a friend's computer hosts tonight's voyage for ten, a community's rented box runs a hundred-sailor sea that never sleeps. How many players fit is the host's call, sized to their machine. This is the oldest multiplayer tradition there is, the one Minecraft proved for fifteen years: worlds live longest when the players own them.

It's also what free forever demands. Your sea never depends on us paying a server bill, so it can never die of one.

Shared official seas take the same idea one step further: each runs on a public meter, its real monthly cost and every coin donated toward it, visible to everyone. The month the meter fills, that sea sails, open to all, donor or not. The month it doesn't, the sea isn't deleted, it freezes: archived exactly as it stood, resuming the day its community wills it back. Money can pause a sea. It can never kill one.

One sailor, many captains

Characters are natives of their sea. Your captain's legend lives in that world's own ledgers, the bounty its marines wrote, the grudges its pirates hold, and none of that can be packed in a crate and shipped to another ocean. So you don't move characters, you collect them: one account, a roster of lives, a hardened veteran on one sea, a fresh nobody on another, ten seconds to switch. Your name and flag travel with you everywhere; your power never does. Sail into a new sea and nobody has heard of you. Earning it again is the game.

One truth, no cheats

The same simulation that runs the whole world runs on the server, and the server is the only voice that counts. Your browser draws your patch of sea and sends your intentions: turn, fire, speak. What actually happens is decided where no player can reach it. A modified game can draw itself a golden ship; it cannot make the server agree.

What a shared sea means

Every system on these pages, unchanged, with people in it. A crew whose captain is a person, whose cook is a person, whose mutiny, if it comes, is personal. A bounty poster other crews actually read. A rival who remembers you because there is genuinely someone remembering. And at the far end of it, the thing this game is being shaped for: two fleets meeting, dozens of captains a side, generals reading the chart above decks where their monsters are dueling. The command ranks on the Crew page were designed for human beings to hold.

While you sleep

A persistent sea keeps sailing after you log off. What happens to your ship that night, safe harbor, loyal crew standing watch, or fair game, is one of the questions we are answering carefully and in the open, before any of this is built. Honest worlds need honest rules about absence.

Where it stands

The most honest badge on the chart: nothing multiplayer runs today, and it sits deliberately last in the build order. But it is no afterthought. The engine is built server-shaped on purpose: the entire simulation already runs headless, fully deterministic, without a renderer attached, in the test suite, today. Every design decision on every other page is audited against one quiet question: will this survive a hundred captains? That's a multiplayer engine waiting for its sea.

One sea. Many captains. The world revolves around none of them.
