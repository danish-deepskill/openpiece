Crew

status: researched, the genre's best studied; the design pass is next

Sailing 1.0 proved one sailor can work a sloop alone. A caravel is another story, and that story is the crew. Shipmates are the mid-game of Openpiece: the moment your ambitions outgrow what two hands can hold, everything starts running through people.

Real people, recruited from a real world

Crewmates aren't spawned menu items, they're characters who already live in the world, with their own skills, quirks, and history. And because you can possess anyone (see Control), your navigator isn't an ability button: step into their boots and read the wind yourself.

You can be the crew

The mast has two sides, and you can stand on either. Hand out ranks aboard your ship, or earn one aboard somebody else's.

Sign onto another captain's ship, a pirate crew, or the Marines proper, recruit to admiral, and their orders land on you as real tasks: stand this watch, run this errand ashore, hold this line when it gets loud. Refuse, and nothing forces your hand, but your captain is a real mind, and he reads you the way you'd read a defiant deckhand. His trust in you is one ledger; the crew's mood is another, because defiance is contagious, and the same murmurs that could one day mutiny against you can start around you. Serve well instead, and you're watching the rise from the bottom rung: learn under a better sailor, earn a name, and step off at some port with a plan of your own.

The crew gates the ship

Big hulls demand hands. Watches to stand, sails to trim, guns to reload, leaks to plug, a caravel without a crew is furniture. Who you recruit decides what your ship can actually do, which makes the crew your real upgrade path.

The roles

The planned role set, and what each one changes about your voyage:

Navigator

Reads wind, currents, and weather you can't see yet. The difference between a route and a guess.

Cook

Meals are morale, and more: what the crew catches becomes what he cooks, and a great meal before a fight is a blessing the whole crew wears. Keep him happy or learn why mutinies start in the galley.

Doctor

Wounds heal right instead of badly. At sea, that's the line between a scar and a burial.

Shipwright

Patches in the fight, refits in port, and one day builds the hull itself. The ship's whole life wears his signature.

Gunner

Cannons, mortars, every gun the ship carries. Reload speed and true aim: a broadside is only as good as the hands behind it.

The officers

Above the trades sits the ship's chain of command, four offices with real jobs:

Captain

Course, fights, contracts. Command at sea is absolute, which is exactly why the rest of this list exists.

First mate

The captain's voice, and the succession: if the captain falls, the ship is theirs. Choosing one is choosing who you become.

Quartermaster

The crew's counterweight, the old pirate way: divides the loot, carries the grievances. When a crew turns on its captain, it turns through the quartermaster. Watch that relationship.

Boatswain

The deck foreman: turns orders into work gangs, rigging into routine. The shipwright crafts; the bosun decides who hauls what today.

The fourth hat: command

Leading is a craft of its own, and it wears bands like any other, earned by leading, and backed by your name, because nobody holds a wing that doesn't respect them:

A brilliant duelist may lead nothing; a quiet cook's-mate may be a born wing-holder, and finding one is finding treasure. When the great fleet battles come, the war has three posts: Squad Leader, Division Commander, Fleet General. None of them is bought, appointed, or granted by a ship's office; they'll be crewmates you grew, fight by fight, band by band.

Four hats, one sailor

Every crewmate is described by four separate things, and keeping them separate is what makes a crew feel real:

  • Office, their rank: where they stand in the chain of command. Captain, first mate, quartermaster, boatswain, or none at all.
  • Profession, their trade: what they've spent years getting good at. Navigator, cook, doctor, shipwright, gunner.
  • Station, their post right now: which chair they're sitting in tonight. The helm, the crow's nest, the dawn watch.
  • Command, their leadership: how many souls hold formation when they point. A hand, a deck, a wing, the sea.

Put together, one sailor reads like this:

Rosa, of the Merry Gale

Office: first mate, second-in-command and your heir. Profession: navigator, the best wind-reader aboard. Station tonight: the helm, dawn watch. Command: a deck, a boarding party follows her without question.

Four different facts, four different systems. Her office says she takes over if you fall. Her profession says the routes are good. Her station says who's steering at sunrise. Her command says she can lead the boarding party while you fight elsewhere. On a five-soul crew, one person wears many hats, like Rosa. On a warship of thirty, every hat has its own head. And the cabin boy? The apprentice office: owns nothing, learns everything.

One law for every role.Three axes each: what they tell you (the navigator calls the wind shift early), what they speed up (the gunner halves the reload), and what they do without being told (a good cook provisions the ship while you're off making enemies). The better the sailor, the more of your ship runs itself, and the more it hurts to lose them.

Life aboard

The intended shape: roles and jobs, watch rotations, provisions that run low, fatigue that builds, morale that remembers how you led. A ship at sea is a small town with no exits, that's the drama this system exists to create.

Where it stands

The research is done, a claim-by-claim study across the genre's best crew systems (RimWorld, Kenshi, and company). The design pass that turns it into Openpiece's own rules is next in the queue. Nothing of it runs in-engine yet, and this page will say so until it does.

The rowboat is yours alone. Everything bigger, you'll owe to people.
