Crew

status: researched — the genre's best studied; the design pass is next

Sailing 1.0 proved one sailor can work a sloop alone. A caravel is another story — and that story is the crew. Shipmates are the mid-game of Openpiece: the moment your ambitions outgrow what two hands can hold, everything starts running through people.

Real people, recruited from a real world

Crewmates aren't spawned menu items — they're characters who already live in the world, with their own skills, quirks, and history. And because you can possess anyone (see Control), your navigator isn't an ability button: step into their boots and read the wind yourself.

You can be the crew

The mast has two sides, and you can stand on either. Sign onto another captain's ship — a pirate crew, or the Marines proper, recruit to admiral — and their orders land on you as real tasks: stand this watch, run this errand ashore, hold this line when it gets loud. Refuse, and nothing forces your hand — but your captain is a real mind, and he reads you the way you'd read a defiant deckhand. His trust in you is one ledger; the crew's mood is another, because defiance is contagious, and the same murmurs that could one day mutiny against you can start around you. Serve well instead, and you're watching the rise from the bottom rung: learn under a better sailor, earn a name, and step off at some port with a plan of your own.

The crew gates the ship

Big hulls demand hands. Watches to stand, sails to trim, guns to reload, leaks to plug — a caravel without a crew is furniture. Who you recruit decides what your ship can actually do, which makes the crew your real upgrade path.

The roles

The planned role set — and what each one changes about your voyage:

  • Navigator — reads wind, currents, and weather you can't see yet; the difference between a route and a guess.
  • Cook — meals are morale; a good one stretches provisions and keeps the galley from becoming the reason for the mutiny.
  • Doctor — wounds heal right instead of badly; at sea, that's the line between a scar and a burial.
  • Shipwright — patches in the fight, refits in port; the hull's lifespan wears his signature.
  • Gunner — reload speed and true aim; a broadside is only as good as the hands behind it.
  • Lookout — sees the sail before it sees you, which decides whether the next hour is an ambush or a choice.

And one law for all of them: every role works on three axes — what they tell you (a navigator calls the wind shift before it hits), what they speed up (a gunner halves the reload), and what they do without being told (a good cook provisions the ship at port while you're off making enemies). The better the sailor, the more of your ship runs itself — and the more it hurts to lose them.

Life aboard

The intended shape: roles and jobs, watch rotations, provisions that run low, fatigue that builds, morale that remembers how you led. A ship at sea is a small town with no exits — that's the drama this system exists to create.

Where it stands

The research is done — a claim-by-claim study across the genre's best crew systems (RimWorld, Kenshi, and company). The design pass that turns it into Openpiece's own rules is next in the queue. Nothing of it runs in-engine yet, and this page will say so until it does.

The rowboat is yours alone. Everything bigger, you'll owe to people.
