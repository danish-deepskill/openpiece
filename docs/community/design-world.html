World

status: built, a living island today; the seamless sea and the clock designed

The world doesn't wait for you. Squads hunt, wildlife grazes and gores, townsfolk defend their own, factions feud, whether you're there or not. You witness more than you trigger. That's the whole ambition of this area, and the first island of it is already alive in-engine.

Our yardstick for "living," in one line: follow anyone, their day should make sense.

The follow test

The designed clock makes days real (a full day passes in about 24 minutes, and night acts through the world, eyes see less, some creatures wake, not through a screen filter). On top of it come schedules: a dockworker with a home, a shift, and a tavern he actually walks to. The schedule system is designed; the daily lives arrive with the crew milestone.

The three-year test

And the long version of that promise: follow one marine for three in-game years, and his life should add up. You'd see the morning drills, because training is real skill growth, not an animation. You might see the promotion, because his organization has real ranks to climb. Far from you, the world runs the same lives in fast, cheap strokes; near you, in full. The two must agree, and because the whole simulation is deterministic, that's a thing we can prove with tests rather than hope.

A world with a clock

The whole sea runs on one always-flowing clock: a full day passes in about twenty-four real minutes, and night is a mechanic, not a lighting filter. Eyes see shorter in the dark, watches rotate, patrol shifts change, some beasts wake, and the night raid becomes a genuine tactic instead of a mood. Dawn is a real deadline. This is the designed substrate half the world's systems stand on, and it's what makes "the world doesn't wait" literal: the clock ticks whether you're watching or not.

One seamless sea

The ocean is a single continuous sea, streamed in chunks, no loading screens between you and the horizon. Distant ships don't vanish; they keep sailing as ledger facts, and stand back up into full ships as you close: crew at stations, fatigue matching the voyage they really made. That sail on the horizon is the abstract world made visible. How all of this is honestly possible, resolutions, ledgers, and the determinism that proves it, has its own page: Simulation.

Islands worth the trip

Every island is hand-built: placed, shaped, and populated by a human who decided what makes that shore worth sailing to, then loaded into the seamless sea as data like everything else. No procedurally-generated island soup; if it's on the chart, somebody meant it. This work is deliberately late: engines first, then the islands that deserve them.

A world that runs without you is the only world worth rising in.
