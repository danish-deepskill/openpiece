Exploration

status: charted — the intent is set; it builds on the sailing that exists

The map starts blank, and filling it is the game. No fog-of-war checklist, no quest marker pointing at the horizon — just a sea that's bigger than what you know about it, and a hundred reasons to fix that.

Charting

What you discover goes on your chart: islands, ports, reefs, the strait that cuts a day off the southern run. And the plan's sharpest idea — charts are items. They can be bought, sold, copied, stolen, and lied about. A good chart of dangerous waters is treasure in its own right.

Finding the way

Navigation is knowledge, not a minimap. Dead reckoning, reading the wind and stars, and — this being a One Piece-inspired sea — the Log Pose, for the waters where an ordinary compass gives up. A veteran navigator sees a route where a rookie sees open water; that gap is the navigator's whole job.

Clues and rumors

Treasure hunting as real puzzling: map fragments that only make sense combined, riddles that point at landmarks, songs with a bearing hidden in the verse, a drunk sailor's rumor that's two-thirds wrong. The challenge isn't reaching the X — it's working out what the clue actually points to.

Where it stands

Charted. The sailing it stands on is built; the knowledge economy (charts, clues, rumors as things you hold and trade) is the design pass to come.

On this sea, knowledge is the treasure.
